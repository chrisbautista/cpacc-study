CPACC · Domain One (40%) · 1B Categories of disability

Cognitive disabilities, part 3: barriers, solutions and assistive technology

Win for today: list the web barriers for cognitive disabilities, match solutions to environments, and match assistive technology to a mental function. Follows part 2.

1. Explain it simply (Feynman technique)

Websites get hard for people with cognitive disabilities when you can't find what you need, forms and passwords are a chore, the page is cluttered, or a timer runs out while you're still thinking. The fixes help everyone: plain words, calm layouts, enough time, a choice of text, audio or pictures, and settings you can tune. The tools are grouped by what part of thinking they prop up (focus, memory, finding your way, feelings, reading and writing, communicating), not by the label on the person.

Explain to your teacher which tool you would suggest for someone who keeps forgetting logins, and which for someone who loses focus mid-task. Where you stumble is the next thing to study.

2. The idea

Web barriers (European Commission study):

  • Finding important information
  • Filling out forms
  • Managing passwords
  • Understanding information
  • Understanding and using controls
  • Keeping focused
  • Information overload (cluttered design, too much text)
  • Time-outs that cause stress and make planning hard

People without cognitive disabilities meet these too, to a lesser degree: they take more time, a person with cognitive disabilities may give up. Features that make a site easier to understand help both groups.

Solutions come in three settings:

  • General: allow adequate time, check understanding and give feedback, minimize background noise, give structure and clear instructions, use plain language
  • Physical environment: key rooms easy to find, simple routes, large clear plain-language signage, simple wayfinding with tactile, graphic, audible or architectural cues
  • ICT: simplified content and distraction-free interfaces, information through different means (text, audio, images), adequate time, highlight what matters, personalized settings

Assistive technology (AT) and adaptive strategies support mental functions. They are not tied to a diagnosis; they assist with specific tasks. Source: BoK Domain One B, pp. 24–26.

Mental functionAT and strategies
Attention and focusingPrompting apps, productivity apps, to-do lists, time and task management apps (break tasks into steps)

5. Mnemonics (ours, not the BoK's)

  • Eight barriers in three groups. Understand it: find information, understand information, understand controls. Do it: forms, passwords. Load: focus, overload, time-outs.
  • Three solution settings: "G-P-I". General, Physical, ICT.
  • AT functions: "AMPERC". Attention, Memory, Perceptual, Emotional, Reading/writing, Communication.
  • Memory pairs: password manager and auto-complete both save you re-remembering. Perceptual has one item only: direction-finding.
  • Numbers, again: dyslexia 5 to 10 (the only range reaching 10), dyscalculia 3 to 6, low vision 246M, hearing loss 430M, and the ladder 200M < 246M < 430M < 2.2B.
