CPACC · Domain Two (40%) · 2C and 2D

WCAG principles and the built environment

Win for today: place any WCAG guideline under its principle, know who benefits beyond disability, and explain why minimum building standards fall short of universal design. This is Domain Two C and D. Follows universal design and benefits.

1. The idea

Source: BoK Domain Two C and D, pp. 39–41. You know WCAG well, so the web part is short: the exam tests the counts and the grouping.

Web accessibility (2C): websites and tools designed so people with disabilities can perceive, understand, navigate, interact with and contribute to the Web. It combines web development standards with universal design practice. It covers these disability groups:

  • Auditory
  • Cognitive
  • Neurological
  • Physical
  • Speech
  • Visual

It also benefits people without disabilities:

  • Small screens and other input modes: mobile phones, smart watches, smart TVs
  • Older people with changing abilities
  • Temporary disabilities: a broken arm, lost glasses
  • Situational limitations: bright sunlight, no way to listen to audio
  • Slow, limited or expensive bandwidth

WCAG 2.2: 4 principles (POUR), 13 guidelines:

PrincipleGuidelinesIn short
Perceivable4Text alternatives, captions, adaptable presentation, easier to see and hear
Operable5Keyboard, enough time, no seizure triggers, navigation, inputs other than keyboard
Understandable3Readable text, predictable behavior, avoid and correct mistakes
Robust1Compatibility with current and future user tools

Version gotcha: the Content Outline says WCAG 2.1; the BoK body text says 2.2. The four principles and 13 guidelines are the same in both, so the counts hold. Following WCAG also makes content more usable in general.

Built environment (2D):

  • Goal: plan, design, build and maintain buildings, public spaces and transport accessible to all people, as disability rates and ageing grow
  • UD began here: the universal design principles were originally developed for the built environment
  • Cost: not building UD in from the start often costs more than the whole construction
  • Minimum standards (vary by country) are about compliance and often amount to accommodation, not UD. Best practice UD guidelines aim for the best result for inclusion.

Areas of focus:

  • Access in and out of buildings (often tied to safety and evacuation)
  • Moving around in buildings: furnishing, facilities, signs, evacuation routes
  • Transport: routes, the outdoor environment, access to transport
  • Recommendations often sit inside broader policies
Exam tells. "4 / 13" and "Robust = compatibility" are WCAG counts. "Sunlight", "broken arm" and "slow connection" are benefits for people without disabilities. "Minimum standard" means compliance and accommodation. "Best practice" means UD. "Originally developed for" means the built environment.

4. Mnemonics (ours, not the BoK's)

  • Guidelines per principle: "4-5-3-1". POUR in order: Perceivable 4, Operable 5, Understandable 3, Robust 1. Total 13.
  • Beyond disability: "S-O-T-S-B". Small screens, Older people, Temporary, Situational, Bandwidth.
  • Minimum = compliance = accommodation. Best practice = universal design.
  • UD was born in buildings, then moved to products and the web.

5. Explain it simply (Feynman technique)

WCAG has four principles: content must be perceivable, operable, understandable and robust. There are 13 guidelines, split 4, 5, 3 and 1. The same fixes help people with temporary or situational limits, older people and people on slow connections. In buildings, the legal minimum usually just lets a few people in, an accommodation. Best practice universal design plans for far more people from the start, and it is cheaper than retrofitting.

Explain to your teacher why a ramp bolted on after construction is usually worse than a step-free entrance planned from day one. Where you stumble is the next thing to study.
