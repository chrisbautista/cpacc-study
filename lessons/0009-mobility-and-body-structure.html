Mobility, flexibility and body structure

Win for today: tell apart the four physical subtypes, recall the 11% figure, and match barriers to solutions. Follows speech and language.

1. The four subtypes

Mobility impairments limit independent, purposeful movement of the body or limbs. They may be temporary or permanent, and present at birth, acquired with age, or the result of disease. Source: BoK Domain One B, pp. 18–20.

2. Solutions and tools to know now

  • Physical: level access and wide entrances; wide unobstructed paths. Tools: walkers, canes, crutches, wheelchairs, scooters, stair lifts, exoskeletons, reachers, grab rails.
  • Digital: big, well-spaced click areas. Tools: switch devices (sip-and-puff), adaptive keyboards, voice control, eye tracking, speech-to-text, head wand, trackball.

5. Mnemonics (ours, not the BoK's)

  • Fingers, Feet, Fatigue, Frame. Fine motor, ambulation, muscle fatigue, body size or shape.
  • Barriers = Reach, Room, Strength, Accuracy.
  • 11% ≈ 1 in 9 US adults have a mobility disability (CDC).
  • Ataxia = lost "a-tax" on fine control. Neurological damage removes fine motor skill.

6. Explain it simply (Feynman technique)

This category is about bodies that move, bend or are built differently. Fingers: doing small things, like buttons or typing. Feet: walking from place to place, with or without a cane or wheelchair. Fatigue: muscles that get exhausted far too easily. Frame: a body whose size or shape doesn't fit things made for an "average" person, like a seat that is too small or a counter that is too high. The problems are mostly about the environment: heavy doors, narrow aisles, tiny buttons. The fixes are wide, level, reachable spaces and big, well-spaced clickable targets. About 1 in 9 US adults has a mobility disability.
