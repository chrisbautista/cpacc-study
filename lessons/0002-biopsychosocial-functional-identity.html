CPACC · Domain One (40%) · 1A Models of disability

The other three models

Win for today: name biopsychosocial, functional solutions and social identity by their tell, and draw the relationship map of all seven models. Builds on Lesson 1.

1. The idea

Same lens idea as before: each model decides where the problem lives. These three are less obvious because they are not "problem in the person" or "problem in society". Source: BoK Domain One A, pp. 5–7.

Dates and bodies. George Engel conceptualized biopsychosocial in 1977. The World Health Organization (WHO) published the International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF) in 2002, derived from it.

Biopsychosocial parts: Bio = physiological pathology. Psycho = thoughts, emotions, behaviours. Social = socio-economic, environmental and cultural factors.

2. The relationship map

Exam favourites, all from the BoK:

  • Charity ← medical + economic (and the BoK says economic is directly related to charity)
  • Biopsychosocial = medical + social, reconciled
  • ICF ← derived from biopsychosocial
  • Functional solutions: the lens for accessibility professionals' work
  • Social identity: most evident among people who are deaf

5. Mnemonics (ours, not the BoK's)

  • Biopsychosocial = Body, Brain, Buddies/community. (Bio, Psycho, Social.)
  • Engel '77 → WHO ICF '02. A quarter-century apart (25 years).
  • Functional solutions = FIX the function. If tech is the answer, it's functional. That's the accessibility job.
  • Social identity = "I belong, I'm proud." Think Deaf culture.
  • Bridge test: biopsychosocial is the bridge between medical and social. Charity is the child of medical and economic.

Explain it simply (Feynman technique)

Biopsychosocial is a bridge between the medical and social views: to understand someone's condition, look at their body, their mind and their world together. Think of a doctor, a counselor and a social worker sitting at one table. Functional solutions is the "just fix it with tools" view: if someone can't do something, invent something that helps. That is basically what accessibility professionals do. Social identity is the "this is my community, and I'm proud" view, strongest in Deaf culture, where a shared language holds people together.
