CPACC · Domain One (40%) · 1B Categories of disability

Disability statistics

Win for today: give the WHO headline figures with their denominators, and state why disability statistics are limited but still useful. This is Domain One D. Follows AT and multiple disabilities.

1. The idea

Disability data is collected and categorized in different ways, so each study must be read in its context. Despite the limits, statistics give background for interventions and a sense of the scale of an issue. Source: BoK Domain One D, pp. 33–34.

Limitations:

  • Census surveys usually don't include details about different disabilities
  • Terminology varies between countries, so conditions are defined differently and are hard to compare
  • Many people have more than one disability and end up between defined groups

Causes of health inequities: unfair conditions such as stigma, discrimination, poverty, exclusion from education and employment, and barriers in the health system itself.

Use of the data: Eurostat says functional status data is a key input for public policy in health, social protection, housing, transport, culture, education and employment. The University of New Hampshire Institute on Disability says statistics can answer:

  • How many people with disabilities live in each country, region or the world
  • What percentages have different types of disability, by age group
  • How many are employed
  • How much disposable income they have
  • How many live in poverty

4. Mnemonics (ours, not the BoK's)

  • "1.3 = 16 = 1 in 6." One number said three ways. It is a share of the whole world.
  • "20, 2, 15": up to 20 years earlier, twice the risk, 15 times harder transport. All are about persons with disabilities.
  • Three limitations: "C-T-M". Census lacks details, Terminology varies, Multiple disabilities fall between groups.
  • Numbers that collide: 16% (1 in 6) of the world has a disability; 10 to 15% of people with disabilities have complex needs; 1 in 12 is red-green color blindness in males. Always ask "of whom?".

5. Explain it simply (Feynman technique)

About 1 in 6 people in the world, 1.3 billion, live with a significant disability. They face worse health, not because of the disability itself but because of unfair conditions: stigma, poverty, exclusion and hard-to-use health services and transport. Counting them is tricky, because countries use different words and categories and many people fit several at once. The numbers are still useful for seeing the size of a problem and planning policy.
