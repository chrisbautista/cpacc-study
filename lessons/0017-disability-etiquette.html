Disability etiquette

Win for today: apply the five interaction guidelines and judge whether a way of referring to a person is people first or identity first. This is Domain One E. Follows disability statistics.

1. The idea

Disability etiquette is a set of guidelines for interacting with and communicating about people with disabilities in an inclusive and respectful way. It covers appropriate behavior, attitudes and respectful language, both in direct communication and when referring to people with disabilities. The BoK study tasks are to follow the guidelines and to judge how appropriate a way of referring to someone is. Source: BoK Domain One E, p. 35.

Personal interactions (five rules):

  • Speak directly to the person, not to the person accompanying them
  • Don't assume what a person can or cannot do
  • Offer help only if the person requests it or after you ask permission, because the impact of a disability varies widely from person to person
  • Acknowledge the person's ability to make their own decisions and judgments
  • Respect personal space and privacy: don't touch a wheelchair or other equipment without permission

Exam tells. "Not the companion", "ask first", "don't touch the wheelchair" are the interaction rules. "Person with a disability" is people first; "disabled person" is identity first. "Ask the person" is the answer when preference is unknown. Scenario questions test judgement: pick the option that is respectful, asks, and doesn't assume.

4. Mnemonics (ours, not the BoK's)

  • Five rules: "D-A-A-A-R". Direct, Assume nothing, Ask before helping, Acknowledge decisions, Respect space.
  • People first = person first in the sentence: "person with a disability". Identity first = disability first: "disabled person".
  • When unsure, ask. It is the right answer to the process questions: offering help, choosing a term for a specific person.
  • Never touch the chair. Equipment is part of personal space.

5. Explain it simply (Feynman technique)

Treat people with disabilities as capable adults. Talk to them, not to whoever is with them. Don't guess what they can or can't do, and don't grab or help unless you've asked and they say yes, because every disability affects people differently. Their wheelchair or cane is part of their personal space. For words, "a person with a disability" puts the person first and is the usual recommendation, but some people prefer "disabled person" because disability is part of who they are. If you aren't sure which they want, ask them.
