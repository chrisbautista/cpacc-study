Deaf-Blindness

Win for today: define Deaf-Blindness, recall its prevalence, and choose the right solution for a scenario. Combines visual and auditory. This quiz is a little harder: more scenarios and mixed review.

1. Explain it simply (Feynman technique)

Deaf-Blind means a person has serious trouble with both hearing and seeing, though most still have a little of each. Since sight and sound can't carry much, touch becomes the main way to communicate. To read, they use braille: raised dots under the fingers. To talk, someone signs into their hands, and they feel the movements. It's rare, somewhere between 2 in 1,000 and 2 in 100 people worldwide. Helpful things: a braille display connected to a computer, braille copies of what's said in videos, and a trained tactile interpreter.

Explain Deaf-Blindness back to your teacher in your own words, including how someone would read and how someone would chat. Where you stumble is the next thing to study.

2. The idea

Deaf-Blindness is a rare sensory disability combining deafness and blindness. It is not just "two disabilities added": the learner's senses for complex communication narrow to touch. Source: BoK Domain One B, pp. 14–15.

TypeDefinitionPrevalence
Deaf-BlindnessBoth deafness and blindness. Most are not completely deaf nor blind. Touch is the only viable channel for complex communication. Braille for text; tactile sign language for conversation (feel the signing hands).0.2% to 2% of the world's population (World Federation of the Deaf-blind)

3. Solutions and tools to know now

  • Braille transcripts of video or audio.
  • Tactile sign language interpretation.
  • Screen reader → refreshable braille device; printed braille; haptic alerts; cane; service animals; tactile navigation aids; Deaf-Blind communicator.

6. Mnemonics (ours, not the BoK's)

  • Touch is the channel. Smell and taste can't carry a conversation.
  • Braille to read, hands to talk. Braille for text, tactile sign for conversation.
  • 0.2 to 2: "a fifth of a percent up to two."
  • Not total, usually. "Some of each": most retain some hearing and sight.
