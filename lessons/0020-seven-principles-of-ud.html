The seven principles of universal design

Win for today: name the seven principles and place any design feature under the right one. This is Domain Two E. Follows WCAG and the built environment.

1. Explain it simply (Feynman technique)

Universal design is good design for everyone. The seven principles: treat everyone the same and don't stigmatize; offer choices; keep it simple and intuitive; present information in several ways; forgive mistakes; need little effort; leave room to reach, see and bring assistive devices. They came from architects, designers and engineers led by Ronald Mace in 1997.

Explain to your teacher how a lever door handle on an automatic door meets at least three of the seven. Where you stumble is the next thing to study.

2. The idea

Source: BoK Domain Two E, pp. 42–43. The seven principles were developed in 1997 by a working group of architects, product designers, engineers and environmental design researchers, led by the late Ronald Mace at North Carolina State University (NCSU). Ireland's Centre for Excellence in Universal Design puts it this way: universal design is the design of an environment so it can be accessed, understood and used to the greatest extent possible by all people, regardless of age, size, ability or disability. It is not a special requirement for a minority. "Simply put, universal design is good design."

PrincipleKey ideas
1. Equitable UseSame means of use for all (identical when possible, equivalent when not). Avoid segregating or stigmatizing. Equal privacy, security and safety. Appealing to all.
2. Flexibility in UseChoice in methods of use. Right- or left-handed access. Facilitate accuracy and precision. Adaptable to the user's pace.
3. Simple and Intuitive UseEliminate unnecessary complexity. Match expectations and intuition. Wide range of literacy and language skills. Information arranged by importance. Effective prompting and feedback.
4. Perceptible InformationDifferent modes (pictorial, verbal, tactile) for redundant presentation. Adequate contrast. Maximize legibility. Elements that can be described. Compatible with devices for sensory limitations.
5. Tolerance for ErrorMost-used elements most accessible; hazards eliminated, isolated or shielded. Warnings. Fail-safe features. Discourage unconscious action where vigilance is needed.
6. Low Physical EffortNeutral body position. Reasonable operating forces. Minimize repetitive actions. Minimize sustained physical effort.
7. Size and Space for Approach and UseClear line of sight, seated or standing. Comfortable reach. Hand and grip size variation. Space for assistive devices.

5. Mnemonics (ours, not the BoK's)

  • The seven in order: "E-F-S-P-T-L-S". Equal, Flexible, Simple, Perceptible, Tolerant, Low-effort, Space. Sentence: "Every Fair Design Puts Thought Lightly Somewhere."
  • Senses in 4: Perceptible = Perception, the sensory one (modes, contrast).
  • Body in 6 and 7: Low Physical Effort is the body's force; Size and Space is the body's reach and room.
  • Safety in 5: Tolerance for Error is the one with hazards, warnings and fail-safe.
