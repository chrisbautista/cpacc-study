Auditory disabilities

Win for today: define deafness, hard of hearing and auditory processing disorder, and recall their numbers. Follows the visual retest.

1. The three types

Auditory disabilities are sensory disabilities ranging from partial to complete hearing loss. Source: BoK Domain One B, pp. 12–14.

TypeDefinitionNumbers
DeafnessTotal or near-total loss of hearing. Born deaf: sign language often first language, text second. Lost later: may prefer text, may never learn sign.—
Hard of hearing (HOH)Mild to severe loss, some useful hearing. May use sign and/or speech, with or without amplification. Most can use the phone and hearing aids.430 million with disabling hearing loss (WHO); 750,000 EU sign-language first-language users
Auditory processing disorder (APD)Difficulty understanding speech despite no measurable hearing loss. Brain, not ears. Confused with ADHD, language impairment, learning disabilities.~5% of the global population

2. Key solutions and tools to know now

  • Live captions: CART or STTR (the BoK gives no expansion; think live captioning).
  • Visual alerts: flashing lights for doorbells and alarms.
  • Sound field systems: amplify a teacher's voice in a classroom.
  • PLD (personal listening devices) connect to assistive listening systems. TTS = text-to-speech software.

Not today: the full barriers and technology lists, and deaf-blindness. They come in later lessons.

5. Mnemonics (ours, not the BoK's)

  • APD: "Ears OK, brain busy." The hearing pathway works; the processing doesn't.
  • 430 million, 750,000, 5%: hearing loss is four-three-oh million; EU signers fit a stadium-sized 750K; APD is 1 in 20.
  • HOH = Has Of Hearing: some useful hearing, can usually use a phone.
  • Born deaf → sign first. Lost later → text first.
  • CART "carts" captions in real time.

6. Explain it simply (Feynman technique)

Deaf means hearing almost nothing. Hard of hearing means hearing some, often with a hearing aid, and most such people can still use a phone. Auditory processing disorder is different: the ears work, but the brain has trouble making sense of what it hears, like a good microphone plugged into a confused computer. Someone born deaf often grew up with sign language, so written words are like a second language. Someone who loses hearing later usually prefers text. Roughly 430 million people have disabling hearing loss, and about 5 in 100 have APD.
