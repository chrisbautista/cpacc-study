CPACC · Domain One (40%) · 1A Models of disability

Spot the model from one sentence

Win for today: given an exam-style scenario, name the model by its tell. Four models, about ten minutes.

1. The idea

A model of disability is a lens: it decides where the problem is said to live. Exam questions give a scenario and ask which lens it shows. Learn the tell, not the textbook definition. No model is fully right; organizations usually blend several, most often medical and social (BoK, Domain One A, pp. 4–7).

Four tells
ModelWhere is the problem?The tell
MedicalIn the person's body: disease, trauma, conditioncure, treat, fix, manage the condition
SocialIn society: barriers, attitudes, policiesremove the barrier, exclusion, inaccessible environment
CharityIn the person's "misfortune"; outsiders helppity, donations, "the less fortunate"
EconomicIn lost ability to work or produceproductivity, benefit eligibility, cost to the state
The steps example (a classic). A wheelchair user can't enter a building because of steps. Medical: the cause is the person's impairment. Social: the cause is the steps.

Links the exam likes: charity is related to both the medical model (disability as an individual problem) and the economic model (disability seen through economic consequences).

Not today: biopsychosocial, functional solutions, and social identity. They get their own lesson, so your working memory stays free.

4. Mnemonics (ours, not the BoK's)

  • Medical = MEND the person. Social = STRIP the barrier.
  • Charity = CHEQUE and pity. If the scenario has a gala or donation drive, it's charity.
  • Economic = EARNINGS. Work, productivity, benefits.
  • Charity is the child of Medical + Economic. One parent for "individual problem", one for "economic consequences".
  • Steps test: blame the person → medical; blame the steps → social.

Explain it simply (Feynman technique)

A model of disability is a pair of glasses for deciding whose job it is to fix the problem. Picture a student who can't get into a building because of steps. Through the medical glasses you say, "her body is the problem, so treat her." Through the social glasses you say, "the steps are the problem, so build a ramp." Charity glasses say, "how sad, let's collect donations." Economic glasses ask, "how much work can she do, and what does it cost?"

Charity is a cousin of both medical and economic: it sees an individual's problem, and it sees the money side.
