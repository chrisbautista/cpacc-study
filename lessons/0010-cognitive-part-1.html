Cognitive disabilities, part 1

Win for today: define cognitive processing, then intellectual disability, dyslexia and dyscalculia with their numbers. ADHD and autism come in part 2. Follows mobility.

1. The idea

Cognitive processes are how a person takes in and interprets information. They use seven mental functions: attention, memory, psychomotor, emotional, perceptual, thought and calculation. A cognitive disability affects one or more of them, to varying degrees. Ability depends on both the person's capacity (internal factors) and context (external factors: information overload, stress, sleep deprivation). Two people with the same diagnosis may not share the same experience. Tools and strategies are grouped by mental function, not diagnosis. Source: BoK Domain One B, pp. 20–23.

4. Mnemonics (ours, not the BoK's)

  • Seven functions: "A Mouse Prefers Eating Pretzels That Crunch." Attention, Memory, Psychomotor, Emotional, Perceptual, Thought, Calculation.
  • ID needs I-A-D: Intellect deficit (IQ below 70–75), Adaptive deficit, Developmental onset.
  • Conceptual = classroom skills; Social = society; Practical = personal care and pay.
  • Count ladder: 200M (intellectual disability) < 246M (low vision) < 430M (hearing loss) < 2.2B (any vision impairment).
  • Percentages: dyslexia 5–10 (up to 17), dyscalculia 3–6: dyscalculia is the smaller number, dyslexia the larger.

5. Explain it simply (Feynman technique)

Your mind has jobs: paying attention, remembering, feeling, seeing patterns, thinking things through and doing sums. A cognitive disability makes one or more of these jobs harder. Stress, too much information or no sleep make it harder for everyone, so good design helps all. Intellectual disability means school-type thinking and everyday life skills (like handling money or making friends) are harder, starting in childhood. About 200 million people have it. Dyslexia means reading is much harder than your intelligence would suggest, and it's quite common: roughly 1 person in 10. Dyscalculia is the same idea with numbers: quantities, times tables and making change feel hard. About 3 to 6 in 100 people.
