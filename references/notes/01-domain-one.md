## 1A. Theoretical Models of Disability

**The framing that matters:** No model is comprehensive, so none is fully correct or incorrect. Each
has strengths and limitations. In practice organizations blend several — most often the social and
medical models.

Exam questions typically give you a **scenario** and ask which model it reflects. Learn the "tell"
for each, not just the definition.

### The seven models

| Model | Core claim | The tell | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Medical** | Disability is a **problem of the person**, caused by disease/trauma; needs treatment or cure | Language about curing, fixing, managing a condition | Addresses real biological sources; medical reality matters to many people | Ignores inaccessible environments and sociopolitical constraints; people report feeling excluded, undervalued, pitied |
| **Social** | **Society** causes disability, not the impairment. Remove barriers → independence and equality | Barriers, exclusion, attitudes, society's failure to accommodate | Shows barriers aren't inevitable; drives environmental and attitudinal change | Can downplay the **embodied** aspects of disability; political activism can create adversaries |
| **Biopsychosocial** | Combines bio + psycho + social. Engel, **1977**. Basis of WHO's **ICF (2002)** | Rehabilitation teams integrating medical and social support | Useful in rehabilitation; bridges the medical/social divide | Fears it defines disability as purely societal, downplaying medical needs; classification is complex |
| **Economic** | Disability = **inability to participate in work**; measures productivity loss and economic consequences | Benefits eligibility, productivity, cost to individual/employer/state | Recognizes real economic support needs | Creates a legally defined "needy" category → stigma; people below the legal threshold get nothing |
| **Functional Solutions** | Identify functional limitations, then **engineer around them** | Technology and methodological innovation as the answer | Results-oriented, practical, service-based | Profit-driven tech can miss the mark; narrow tech focus ignores social/political fixes |
| **Social Identity / Cultural Affiliation** | Identity comes from **membership in a group**. Most evident in **Deaf culture** | Pride, community, linguistic minority | Accepts disability fully; source of pride | Belonging for insiders can mean exclusion for those who don't fit the group |
| **Charity** | People with disabilities are **unfortunate and need outside help**; givers are benevolent | Pity, donations, "helping the less fortunate" | Can mobilize genuine help and resources | Condescending; creates dependence on pity; short-term fixes crowd out long-term solutions |

### Relationships between models — often tested
- **Charity** is related to both the **medical** model (disability as individual problem) and the
  **economic** model (disability framed in economic consequences).
- **Biopsychosocial** is the deliberate attempt to reconcile **medical + social**.
- The **ICF** (WHO, 2002) is *derived from* the biopsychosocial model.
- The **functional solutions** model is the lens through which the work of accessibility
  professionals is most naturally viewed.
- The shift from **medical → social** model is the main driver of the legal shift from welfare/social-security
  law toward **anti-discrimination/equality law** (this links forward to Domain Three).

**Classic exam trap — the steps example:** A wheelchair user can't enter a building because of steps.
*Medical model:* the problem is the person's impairment. *Social model:* the problem is the steps.

## 1B Categories and Characteristics of Disabilities, Associated Barriers, and Solutions; and - 1C. Identify Appropriate Assistive Technologies and Adaptive Strategies at the Level of the Individual for Permanent, Temporary and Episodic Disabilities (ICT and Physical World)

### Three definitions you must be able to distinguish

- **Assistive technologies** — products, devices, systems, or items used by people with disabilities
  to perform tasks they could not do otherwise. Also called adaptive technologies/software when used
  with computers. **Not all are computer-based** — a cardboard communication board counts. Some rely
  on the output of other **user agents** (browsers, voice browsers, media players, plug-ins).
- **Adaptive strategies** — tweaks and adjustments *people themselves* make to perform daily
  activities. Software settings, or environmental changes like moving closer to a speaker.
- **Accessibility** — designing products, services, and environments for equal access. **The
  provider, producer, or owner is responsible** for providing accessibility measures.

> The distinction that gets tested: AT is a *thing you use*; an adaptive strategy is a *thing you do*;
> accessibility is a *responsibility of the provider*.

### 1. Visual Disabilities (sensory)

Covers vision loss, loss of **visual acuity** (sharpness), altered color sensitivity, and complete or
uncorrectable loss in either or both eyes.

| Type | Definition | Key numbers |
|---|---|---|
| **Blindness** | Some, near-complete, or complete vision loss. Some perceive light/dark or large shapes but can't read text or recognize faces | **2.2 billion** globally have vision impairment or blindness; **≥1 billion** of those were preventable or unaddressed. Leading causes: **uncorrected refractive errors and cataracts**. Most are **over 50** |
| **Color vision deficiency** | Can't distinguish certain color combinations | **Red-green** is most common: **1 in 12 males (8.3%)**, **1 in 200 females (0.5%)**. **Blue-yellow** affects sexes equally, **fewer than 1 in 10,000** worldwide |
| **Low vision** | *Functional* definition: uncorrectable vision loss that interferes with daily activities — "not enough vision to do whatever it is you need to do." Not correctable by glasses, contacts, medicine, or surgery | **246 million**, **3.5%** of world population. **~90%** of people with vision impairments live in low-income settings |

**Barriers:** no alternate formats (digital, braille); people who don't describe visual information;
inadequate lighting; sounds masking directional cues; non-tactile signs; obstacles in travel paths;
missing text alternatives; content that breaks when resized; missing orientation cues and page
structure; video without text/audio alternatives or audio description; inconsistent navigation;
insufficient color contrast; tools that block custom color combinations; anything not keyboard-operable.

**Solutions — physical:** raised/tactile tiles marking platform edges, pathways, stair starts;
eliminate low-hanging architectural features; clear hallway and sidewalk obstructions; braille on
signs and controls (elevator buttons, code locks); tactile controls on flat appliances.

**Solutions — ICT:** text alternatives for non-text content; design that allows magnification; high
contrast; **never rely on color alone**; standard, consistent positioning of objects.

**Assistive technologies:** screen readers (convert text *and structural information* to speech);
**audio description** (extra audio track describing essential visual information); screen
magnification; large print; audio GPS navigation (automated or remote human navigator); apps that
describe photographed objects; barcode/QR scanners that speak product info; color contrast/filter/theme
software; canes; service animals.

### 2. Auditory Disabilities (sensory)

| Type | Definition | Key numbers |
|---|---|---|
| **Deafness** | Total or near-total hearing loss | — |
| **Hard of hearing (HOH)** | Mild-to-severe loss with **some useful hearing** remaining; may use sign and/or spoken language, with or without amplification. Most can use the phone and hearing aids | **430 million** with disabling hearing loss (WHO). **750,000** in the EU use sign language as a first language |
| **Central Auditory Processing Disorder (APD)** | Greater-than-expected difficulty understanding speech **despite no measurable hearing loss**. Not an inability to hear — an inability to **interpret, organize, or analyze** what's heard. The hearing pathway works; parts of the brain don't | **~5%** of the global population |

**Language nuance that gets tested:** people **born deaf** often have **sign language as a first
language** and may be less comfortable with text (phonetic notation doesn't help them). People who
lose hearing **later in life** may never learn sign and often prefer text.

**APD is frequently confused with** ADHD, language impairment, learning disabilities, social/emotional
delays, and cognitive deficits.

**Barriers:** soft speakers or large spaces without microphones; no sign language interpretation;
loud environments and competing background noise; multi-speaker meetings; poor lighting that prevents
lip-reading; tech requiring voice interaction; audio without captions or transcripts; media players
lacking caption support or caption size/color/volume controls.

**Solutions:** sign language interpretation; **CART or STTR** accurate and usable captions for video,
live online meetings, and live presentations; transcripts; **visual alerts** for doorbells and alarms
(flash, pulse, dim, on/off); quiet work environments; clear signage for rooms with **audio induction
loops**; good acoustics and lighting.

**Assistive technologies:** assistive listening systems in meeting rooms and auditoria; **personal
listening devices (PLD)**; hearing aids; cochlear implants; audio controls; haptic alerts; visual
labels/notifications; text-to-speech (TTS); **sound field systems** (amplify a teacher's voice in a
classroom); noise-canceling headphones.

### 3. Deaf-Blindness (sensory)

Both deafness and blindness. **Most Deaf-Blind people are not completely deaf nor completely blind** —
they retain some hearing and sight. Meaningful sensory input narrows to touch, smell, and taste, and
**touch is the only viable channel for complex communication**.

Typically learns **braille** for text and **tactile sign language** for conversation — feeling the
signing hands of the other person.

**Prevalence: between 0.2% and 2%** of the world's population (World Federation of the Deafblind).

**Barriers:** no printed braille for books/menus/navigation aids; incorrect or incomplete output to a
braille keyboard; no braille transcripts of video or audio; no tactile sign language interpretation.

**Assistive technologies:** screen reader converting text to braille on a **refreshable braille
device**; transcript-to-braille conversion; printed braille; haptic feedback; cane; service animals;
tactile navigation aids; tactile sign language interpretation; **Deaf-Blind communicator**.

### 4. Speech and Language Disabilities

**The core distinction:** *Language* is understanding, formulating, and sharing ideas through words —
language disorders can affect writing, reading, and comprehension too. *Speech* disorders concern
specifically **how people say words and make sounds**.

Critically: **the ability to physically speak may be completely unrelated to language capability.**
Someone may read, write, and understand perfectly while mouth structure or neuromuscular connections
prevent speech.

| Type | Definition | Key numbers |
|---|---|---|
| **Organic speech sound disorders** | Result from **motor/neurological** causes: **apraxia of speech** (knows what to say, brain struggles to plan the movements), **dysarthria** (difficulty controlling speech muscles), **structural deficiencies** (cleft lip/palate), **sensory/perceptual** (e.g. hearing loss) | Speech sound disorders overall: **5–25% of children** (varies by age group), **1–2% of adults** |
| **Functional speech sound disorders** | **No known cause** — not acquired, not developmental. Errors in **articulation** (clear distinct sounds) and **phonology** (sound patterns) | (as above) |
| **No speech / mutism** | Inability to speak. **Neurogenic** = brain injury (often the extreme end of aphasia, apraxia, or dysarthria). **Psychogenic** = psychological cause | Selective mutism: **0.47%–0.76%**. Neurogenic mutism: no statistics available |
| **Aphasia** | Language disorder from **neurological damage** — affects production *and* comprehension of speech, and the ability to read or write. **Always** due to brain injury; most commonly **stroke** (especially in older people), also head trauma, tumors, infections | **≥2,000,000** in the USA; **≥250,000** in Great Britain; global rate unknown |

**Three types of psychogenic mutism — memorize the distinction:**
- **Elective** — the person **chooses** not to speak
- **Selective** — the person **wants to speak but cannot** in certain situations, due to anxiety
- **Total** — the person does not speak at all

**Barriers:** complex communication systems; not enough time to communicate or respond; lack of
understanding and patience from others; no multimodal or text-based alternatives to speech.

**Assistive technologies:** **AAC (Augmentative and Alternative Communication) devices**;
text-to-speech; screen readers (adjustable rate, voice, pitch); keyboards with speech generation;
electronic communication boards with symbols/images; **voice carryover**; writing templates,
organizational tools, word prediction, spell checkers; speech-to-text; speech practice apps.

### 5. Mobility, Flexibility, and Body Structure Disabilities

Covers upper/lower limb loss or disability, manual dexterity challenges, coordination disability
across body organs, and broken skeletal structure. May be **temporary or permanent**; present at
birth, acquired with age, or caused by disease.

| Type                                      | Definition                                                                                                                                | Key numbers                                                                                                                                                                          |
| ----------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Manual dexterity / fine motor control** | Intricate hand and wrist movements to manipulate and control objects. Built on **gross** motor skills. Temporary, recurring, or permanent | No direct statistics. Affects older adults, people with autism or ADHD, and people with **ataxia** (loss of fine motor skills from neurological damage — stroke, cerebral palsy, MS) |
| **Ambulation**                            | Ability to walk from place to place independently, **with or without an assistive device** (Nursing Outcomes Classification)              | No specific stats. **11% of US adults** have mobility disabilities (CDC); similar in Europe and Canada                                                                               |
| **Muscle fatigue**                        | Overwhelming tiredness, lack of energy, exhaustion; difficulty performing voluntary tasks. **Initial sign is muscle weakness**            | No estimates — too many causes (illness, age, medication, inactivity, depression)                                                                                                    |
| **Body size or shape**                    | Disorders affecting stature, proportions, or shape: **acromegaly, dwarfism, rheumatoid arthritis, obesity**                               | No estimates — definitions vary                                                                                                                                                      |

**Barriers — physical:** seating too small or at wrong height; appliances/controls out of reach or
requiring touch instead of voice; narrow walkways, doorways, aisles; tasks requiring fine motor skill
(small round door handles); tasks requiring accuracy (small buttons, switches, dials); tasks requiring
strength (heavy doors); high shelves and counters; tables without **knee and toe clearance**; ATMs and
equipment requiring a standing position; steps and thresholds; **body shaming and social discrimination**.

**Barriers — digital:** interfaces requiring one specific input (keyboard-only or mouse-only); small
and/or tightly grouped touch targets.

**Assistive technologies — physical:** walkers, canes, crutches; manual and electric wheelchairs,
motorized scooters; stair lifts, elevators; **exoskeletons**; stepladders; grab rails and handlebars;
reachers; touch- or voice-operated light fixtures.

**Assistive technologies — ICT:** switch devices replacing keyboard/mouse (**sip-and-puff**);
adaptive/customizable keyboards; voice control; **eye tracking**; speech-to-text; **head wand**;
oversized mouse or trackball; adjustable-position displays.

### 6. Cognitive Disabilities

**Cognitive processes** are how a person takes in and interprets information. The **mental functions**
involved — worth memorizing the list:

> Attention · Memory · Psychomotor · Emotional · Perceptual · Thought · Calculation

**A structural point the BoK makes explicitly and that gets tested:** cognitive processes are affected
by **internal** factors (impairments in mental functions) *and* **external** factors (information
overload, stress, sleep deprivation). Stress is highly disruptive to working memory **for people with
and without cognitive impairments**. So a person's ability to understand information depends on both
their capacity *and* the broader context.

**And the key design implication:** AT and adaptive strategies for cognitive disabilities are **not
developed for a diagnosis — they support a specific mental function.** Barriers and strategies group
by *mental function*, not by diagnosis.

| Type | Definition | Key numbers |
|---|---|---|
| **Intellectual disability** | Significant limitations in **intellectual functioning** (reasoning, learning, problem solving) **and adaptive behavior**. DSM-V term; some countries call this "learning disabilities" | **~200 million** worldwide (Special Olympics) |
| **Dyslexia** | Learning disability affecting reading; read significantly below expectation **despite normal intelligence**. Difficulty with **phonological processing**, spelling, rapid visual verbal responding. Can be inherited | **5–10%** of the population, possibly as high as **17%**. **70–80%** of people with reading difficulties likely have some form. Affects boys and girls similarly |
| **Dyscalculia** | Inability to understand arithmetic and how to calculate. Complicated by **dysgraphia** (inability to draw or copy figures and graphs) and anxiety | **3–6%** of people |
| **ADHD** | Two symptom groups: **inattention/distraction** and **hyperactive/impulsive** | Children **2–7%** globally; adults **~4%**. **2–3 in 10** people with ADHD have attention/concentration challenges *without* impulsiveness or hyperactivity |
| **Autism spectrum disorders** | Group of complex brain development disorders: difficulties in social interaction and communication + restricted, repetitive interests and activities | **~1 in 100** people (Autism Europe) |
| **Non-Verbal Learning Disability (NLD)** | Normal intelligence and language development but trouble with social skills, sensory input, and transitions. Very much like Asperger Syndrome — same kind of disorder, differing in **severity** (AS = more severe) | **~1 in 100 (1%)** of US children. Affects boys and girls about equally |

**Three DSM-V criteria for intellectual disability:**
1. Deficits in **intellectual functions** — typically **IQ below 70–75**
2. Impairments in **adaptive behaviour** — social, communication, and school/work management skills
3. Impairments **manifest during the developmental years** (childhood). Same condition after adult
   head trauma = a different diagnosis

**Three categories of adaptive behavior (AAIDD):**
- **Conceptual** — language, reading, writing, managing time, managing numbers, reasoning, knowledge, memory
- **Social** — interpersonal skills, social judgment and responsibility, communication, following rules, making and keeping friendships
- **Practical** — personal care, job responsibilities, schedules, safety, travel and transportation, managing money, organizing school and work tasks

**ADHD diagnostic thresholds:** children need **6+** symptoms in a category; adults need **5+**.
- *Inattention/distraction:* diminished attention span; distracted by surrounding stimuli; difficulty
  starting/organizing/finishing tasks; forgetfulness, losing or misplacing items.
- *Hyperactive/impulsive:* restlessness and difficulty controlling it; difficulty remaining seated;
  fidgeting; difficulty waiting turns; overly talkative, interrupting.

**Two DSM-V criteria for autism spectrum disorders:**
1. Restrictions in **social communication and social interaction**, present in **different contexts**
   (home *and* school/work)
2. **Repetitive behaviours** — strict routines, repetitive movements

Plus: symptoms must significantly affect functioning, must have **occurred during the developmental
period**, and must not be better explained by another condition. **Asperger Syndrome is no longer a
separate diagnosis** — it's classified as a form of ASD. Diagnosis has **3 levels of severity**.
Autism occurs **with and without** intellectual disability.

**Barriers (from the EU study on cognitive web accessibility):** finding important information;
filling out forms; managing passwords; understanding information; understanding and using controls;
keeping focused; **information overload** from cluttered design or too much text; **time-outs** that
cause stress and disrupt planning.

> The BoK makes a point worth remembering: people **without** cognitive disabilities experience these
> same barriers, just to a lesser degree. The difference is that someone without a cognitive
> disability takes longer, while someone with one **may give up**. Both groups benefit from the fixes.

**Solutions — general:** adequate time to prepare and absorb; check for understanding and give
feedback; minimize background noise; clear structure and instructions; **plain language or easy-to-read**.

**Solutions — physical:** key rooms easy to find; simple, intuitive accessible routes; large, clear,
plain-language signage; simple wayfinding with tactile, graphic, audible, or architectural cues.

**Solutions — ICT:** simplified content; distraction-free interfaces; information through **different
means** (text, audio, images); adequate time; highlight the most important information; personalized
settings for layout, time management, content.

**Assistive technologies, grouped by mental function:**
- **Attention/focusing** — prompting applications to stay on task, productivity apps, to-do lists, time management apps, task-breakdown apps
- **Memory** — audio prompting devices, form auto-complete, **password managers**, audio note-takers and reminders, action-cue apps
- **Perceptual** — direction-finding applications
- **Emotional** — mindfulness and stress-reduction apps, anxiety management and coping apps
- **Reading and writing** — audio books and reading software, speech synthesizers/screen readers, word prediction/lookup, visual and audio alternatives to text in signage, speech-recognition software
- **Communication** — AAC devices, **synchronized speech and highlighting**, computerized voice output communication aids, computer-assisted instruction

### 7. Seizure Disabilities

A **seizure** is a sudden, uncontrolled electrical disturbance in the brain, causing changes in
behavior, movements, feelings, or consciousness. **Two or more seizures, or a tendency to recurrent
seizures = epilepsy.**

- **50 million** people have epilepsy globally — one of the most common neurological diseases.
- **Photosensitive epilepsy:** seizures triggered by flashing/flickering lights or patterns.
  **~3% of people with epilepsy.** Two groups: those with *pure photosensitivity* (only triggered
  seizures) and those who also have seizures at other times.

**The numbers to memorize:**
- Flash/flicker rates between **16 and 25 times per second** are most likely to trigger seizures.
- Some people are sensitive to rates as low as **3** or as high as **60** per second.
- **High contrast** patterns and **moving** patterns are more likely to trigger seizures.
- **Accessibility rule:** nothing should flash **more than three times in any one-second period**,
  or the flash must be below the **general flash and red flash thresholds**.

**Barriers:** activities where sudden loss of consciousness causes harm (swimming, bathing, power
tools); moving, blinking, or flickering content; browsers and media players lacking controls to stop
or turn off video and animations.

**Assistive technologies:** mobile digital diary apps with reminders; **smart watches that detect
seizures** and send alerts or GPS location; service animals; wearables with an alert button; supportive
and protective gear for falls; **flicker-free monitors**; monitor glare guards; non-glare glasses.

### 8. Psychological Disabilities

Affect perception, thoughts, feelings, mood, and behavior. **Occasional or long-lasting.** Three groups:

- **Anxiety disorders** — including panic disorders and phobias
- **Mood disorders** — including depression and bipolar disorder
- **Psychotic disorders** — including schizophrenia

| Condition | Key facts | Numbers |
|---|---|---|
| **Anxiety disorders** | More than temporary worry or fear; doesn't go away and can worsen | Prevalence varies **2.5–7%** by country. **284 million** in 2017 — the **most prevalent mental health disorder** |
| **Generalized Anxiety Disorder** | Persistent anxiety or dread for **months, if not years**. Restless/wound-up/on-edge, difficulty concentrating, easily fatigued, sleep problems, unexplained aches | — |
| **Panic disorder** | Frequent, unexpected **panic attacks** — sudden intense fear or loss of control with no clear trigger. **Not everyone who has a panic attack develops panic disorder** | — |
| **Social anxiety disorder** | Intense, persistent fear of **being watched and judged** | — |
| **Bipolar disorder** | Extreme mood swings; episodes of **depression** and episodes of **mania**. Unlike ordinary mood swings, **each episode may last weeks**. Both types may involve delusions, hallucinations, disturbed thinking | **19 million** worldwide in 2019 (WHO) |
| **Psychotic disorders** | Affect thinking and perception; people **lose touch with reality**. **Delusions** = believing untrue things. **Hallucinations** = perceiving things that aren't there | **Schizophrenia: 24 million, or 1 in 300** worldwide (WHO) |

Most common mood disorders: **depression, bipolar disorder, seasonal affective disorder, self-harm**.
Psychotic symptoms can also occur in bipolar disorder, or from stroke, brain tumors, or alcohol/drug
addiction.

**Barriers:** limited availability and affordability of mental healthcare; lack of provider knowledge
for accurate diagnosis and treatment; **social stigma**; lack of support for cognitive challenges;
for students, lack of emotional/behavioural/cognitive support at school.

**Solutions:** because psychological disabilities affect mental functions and cognition, **many
cognitive-disability solutions apply here too**.

**Assistive technologies:** mood/stress/anxiety management apps; memory aids; text-to-speech; reminder
devices; voice recognition software; noise monitoring devices.

### 9. Multiple / Complex Disabilities

More than one disability present at the same time — physical, mental, or a combination. In education,
this category covers students with the most profound disabilities.

**Jurisdictional note:** in some countries, including the US, the definition of multiple disabilities
**excludes deaf-blindness**, because that has its own special classification in legislation (e.g. for
education).

**Five developmental areas showing deficits** (ProjectIDEAL, children with multiple disabilities):
intellectual functioning · adaptive skills · motor skills · sensory functioning · communication skills

**Prevalence:** no global statistics. UK and Australian sources indicate **10–15% of people with
disabilities have complex needs.**

**Assistive technologies:** programmable keyboards; writing support tools; communication aids;
**text-to-braille translation software**; other adaptive equipment.

## 1D. Disability Demographics and Statistics

### The three limitations of disability statistics — memorize these
1. **Details about different disabilities are usually not included in census surveys.**
2. **Terminology varies between countries**, so conditions may be defined differently and comparison is hard.
3. **Many people have more than one disability** and end up between defined groups.

Despite the limits, statistics remain useful as background for interventions and for conveying the
scale of an issue.

### WHO headline figures
- **1.3 billion** people experience significant disability — **16% of the world's population, 1 in 6.**
- Some people with disabilities die **up to 20 years earlier**.
- **Twice the risk** of developing depression, asthma, diabetes, stroke, obesity, or poor oral health.
- Find inaccessible and unaffordable transportation **15 times more difficult** than people without disabilities.
- Health inequities arise from stigma, discrimination, poverty, exclusion from education and
  employment, and barriers within the health system itself.

> Note the tension: Domain Three A of the BoK says people with disabilities are "approximately 15%
> of the world's population — the world's largest minority," while Domain One D leads with the newer
> WHO figure of **16% / 1.3 billion**. Both appear in the source. If a question offers both, prefer
> **16% / 1.3 billion / 1 in 6** for prevalence, and remember **"world's largest minority"** as the
> phrase attached to the 15% framing.

**Why the data is collected:** functional-status data is a key input for public policy in health,
social protection, housing, transport, culture, education, and employment (Eurostat). It answers
questions about how many people with disabilities live in each country/region, percentages by
disability type and age group, how many are employed, disposable income, and how many live in poverty
(University of New Hampshire Institute on Disability).

## 1E. Disability Etiquette

**Definition:** guidelines for interacting with and communicating **about** people with disabilities
in an inclusive and respectful way — behavior, attitudes, and language, in both direct communication
and reference.

### The five etiquette rules
1. **Speak to the person directly**, not to the person accompanying them.
2. **Do not make assumptions** about what a person can or cannot do.
3. **Provide assistance only if requested**, or after asking permission — impact varies widely person to person.
4. **Acknowledge the person's ability to make their own decisions and judgments.**
5. **Respect personal space and privacy** — don't touch a wheelchair or other equipment without permission.

### Person-first vs. identity-first language — high-probability exam topic

- **Person-first** ("a person with a disability") — the most common recommendation. Emphasis on the
  **person, not the disability**, to avoid labelling and stigmatization. This is the form used in the
  **UN CRPD**, and **G3ict/IAAP use it** in line with the UNCRPD.
- **Identity-first** ("a disabled person") — preferred by some people with disabilities, often
  **self-advocates within disability communities**. Emphasis on disability as a **key part of identity**.

**The rule that resolves it:** in direct communication, or when referring to a specific person,
**ask the person what they prefer.** Neither form is universally correct.
