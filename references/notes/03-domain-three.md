## 3A. International Declarations and Conventions

### The framing

People with disabilities make up **approximately 15% of the world's population — the world's largest
minority.** Yet they have only recently been protected in international human rights law: they are
**not listed** among the groups protected against discrimination in the **1948 Universal Declaration
of Human Rights**.

**Three things a legal instrument can do** (worth memorizing — it structures the whole domain):
1. **Grant fundamental or human rights to every person**, including people with disabilities
2. **Prohibit discrimination based on disability** — either listing it explicitly, or under a general
   category like "**other status**"
3. **Directly address the rights of people with disabilities**

**Definition of discrimination** (Inter-American Convention, Art. 1(2)(a)): any distinction,
exclusion, or restriction based on a disability, **record of** disability, condition resulting from a
**previous** disability, or **perception of** disability, **whether present or past**, which has the
effect or objective of impairing or nullifying the recognition, enjoyment, or exercise of human rights
and fundamental freedoms.

### The 1975 Declaration on the Rights of Disabled Persons

Passed by the UN in **1975**. Because it was a **declaration, not a convention**, it could only
provide a **framework and recommendations** — not binding obligations.

It asserts that people with disabilities have the **same civil and political rights as others**,
declares the need to protect them against exploitation and discriminatory, abusive, or degrading
treatment, recognizes the need to help them develop their abilities, and promotes integration into
normal life — **but** accepts that "certain countries, at their present stage of development, can
devote only limited efforts to this end."

> **Declaration vs. convention is the key distinction in this section.** A declaration frames and
> recommends. A convention binds.

### 1. Universal Declaration of Human Rights (UDHR), 1948

A **milestone document**. Drafted by representatives with different legal and cultural backgrounds
from all regions of the world. Set out, **for the first time**, fundamental human rights to be
universally protected.

**Its role:** the **foundational document** for the CRPD and for all other human rights conventions,
proclaiming civil, cultural, economic, political, and social rights to be for all peoples.

**But:** disability is **not** listed among its protected groups.

### 2. Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (CRPD), 2006

**Why it exists:** developed in response to concerns that there was **no legally binding convention**
protecting and recognizing the rights of people with disabilities in existing human rights instruments.

**The paradigm shift it represents** — quotable and highly testable: it moves from viewing persons
with disabilities as **"objects" of charity, medical treatment, and social protection** toward viewing
them as **"subjects" with rights**, capable of claiming those rights and making decisions for their
lives based on **free and informed consent**, and being **active members of society**.

**Key facts:**
- **Adopted 2006.** A human rights instrument with an explicit **social development dimension**.
- **The first binding international human rights instrument specifically addressing the rights of
  people with disabilities.** Beyond recognizing rights, **states parties must take steps to implement them.**
- Adopts a **broad categorization** of persons with disabilities; reaffirms that all persons with all
  types of disabilities must enjoy all human rights and fundamental freedoms.
- **Over 160** countries and regional integration organisations **signed**; **more than 180 ratified**.
- The **basis and reference point** for national disability rights instruments in states parties.
- **EU note:** the EU **signed as a block**, but **EU Member States ratified individually**, as did the
  EU. So the EU implements and reports on the Convention at **both EU and national levels**.

**Monitoring mechanism:**
- States parties must establish a framework to **promote, protect, and monitor** implementation.
- The **Committee on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities** regularly reviews implementation,
  informed by organisations representing people with disabilities.
- The Committee compiles a **List of Issues**, considering written input from **NGOs**. The questions
  in the List of Issues form the basis for states parties' reports.
- **In parallel, NGOs can submit alternative reports.**

**Accessibility in the CRPD:**
- Listed as a **General principle in Article 3**
- Detailed as a right in **Article 9 — Accessibility**

**Article 9 in brief:** states parties shall ensure access **on an equal basis with others** to the
physical environment, transportation, information and communications (including ICT), and other
facilities and services open to the public, in **both urban and rural areas**, including:
- (a) buildings, roads, transportation, other indoor and outdoor facilities — schools, housing, medical facilities, workplaces
- (b) information, communications, and other services, including electronic and emergency services

And states parties shall also: develop and monitor **minimum standards and guidelines**; ensure
**private entities** offering public facilities/services account for accessibility; provide **training
for stakeholders**; provide **signage in Braille and in easy-to-read forms**; provide **live assistance
and intermediaries** (guides, readers, professional sign language interpreters); promote access to
information; promote access to **new ICT including the Internet**; and promote accessible ICT design
**at an early stage, so these technologies become accessible at minimum cost**.

### 3. The Marrakesh Treaty, 2013

**Full name:** the Marrakesh Treaty to Facilitate Access to Published Works for Persons who are Blind,
Visually Impaired, or Otherwise Print Disabled.

- **Adopted June 27, 2013**, in Marrakesh. Administered by **WIPO** (World Intellectual Property
  Organization) as part of the body of international **copyright** treaties.
- **Purpose:** end the "**book famine**" faced by people with print disabilities.
- **Mechanism:** creates a **copyright exception** — a set of **mandatory limitations and exceptions**.
  People with print disability and their organizations **do not need to ask permission** to create
  copyrighted print materials in accessible formats and distribute them to others with print disability.
- Also allows the **import and export** of accessible format versions.

> The Marrakesh Treaty is the odd one out: it's a **copyright** treaty, not a human rights convention.
> That's why it's memorable — and why it's a good distractor in exam questions.

## 3B. Regional Instruments

**Why regional instruments still matter after the CRPD:** they can **reflect and address regional
issues**, **fill gaps**, and **strengthen monitoring and enforcement**. They also serve as a basis to
bring complaints before **regional human rights tribunals** — the European Court of Human Rights, the
Inter-American Court of Human Rights, and the African Court on Human and Peoples' Rights.

### 1. Europe — three layered instruments

| Instrument | Year | Body | What it does |
|---|---|---|---|
| **European Convention on Human Rights (ECHR)** | **1950** | Council of Europe | Grants fundamental **civil and political** rights to all. Any person can take a violation to the **European Court of Human Rights**. Disability is **not explicitly listed** — covered under "**other status**" |
| **European Social Charter (ESC)** | **1961** | Council of Europe | Guarantees fundamental **social and economic** rights as the **counterpart to the ECHR**. Grants people with disabilities the right to **independence, social integration, and participation in community life** |
| **EU Charter of Fundamental Rights** | **2000** | European Union | Brings together the fundamental rights of everyone living in the EU. Introduced for **consistency and clarity** across rights established at different times in different Member States |

**The Charter's five sources** — it is based on:
1. Rights and freedoms recognised by the **European Convention on Human Rights**
2. The **constitutional traditions** of EU Member States
3. The Council of Europe's **Social Charter**
4. The **Community Charter of Fundamental Social Rights of Workers**
5. Other international conventions to which the EU or its Member States are parties

**Became legally binding** on EU Member States when the **Treaty of Lisbon** entered into force in
**December 2009**.

**Two articles to memorize:**
- **Article 26 — Integration of persons with disabilities:** the Union recognises and respects the right
  of persons with disabilities to benefit from measures designed to ensure their **independence, social
  and occupational integration, and participation in the life of the community**.
- **Article 21 — Non-discrimination:** prohibits discrimination on any ground including sex, race,
  colour, ethnic or social origin, genetic features, language, religion or belief, political or any
  other opinion, membership of a national minority, property, birth, **disability**, age, or sexual orientation.

### 2. African Charter on Human and People's Rights, 1981

- Adopted **1981**; recognizes the rights of all people.
- **Does not specify disability** when talking about non-discrimination — but its provisions **have
  been used** to fight disability discrimination and provide equal protection.
- **2018:** the African Union adopted the **African Disability Rights Protocol (ADRP)**, a Protocol to
  the Charter focused on safeguarding the human rights of people with disabilities.

**What the ADRP adds beyond the CRPD:**
- Specific provisions about **armed conflicts and forced displacements**
- More extensive provisions on **harmful practices**, listing some of the most common ones
- **ADRP Article 11(1)** requires measures and support for victims of harmful practices — including
  legal sanctions and educational and advocacy campaigns — to eliminate practices such as **witchcraft,
  abandonment, concealment, ritual killings, or the association of disability with omens**
- (Compare **CRPD Article 8(1)(b) — Awareness-raising:** combat stereotypes, prejudices, and harmful
  practices relating to persons with disabilities, including those based on sex and age, in all areas of life)

**Status:** as of mid-2023, the ADRP had **not yet reached the 15 ratifications** necessary to come
into force.

### 3. Inter-American Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination Against Persons with Disabilities, 1999

- Adopted **1999 in Guatemala**, **before the CRPD**.
- **The first regional, binding treaty that expressly prohibits discrimination against people with
  disabilities.** ← this superlative is the most testable fact here.
- **Article II objectives:** prevent and eliminate all forms of discrimination against persons with
  disabilities, and promote their **full integration into society**.
- **Article III** addresses accessibility directly. Measures include:
  - **Gradually eliminating** discrimination and promoting integration by making available goods,
    services, facilities, programs, and activities — employment, transportation, communications,
    housing, recreation, education, sports, law enforcement and administration of justice, and
    political and administrative activities
  - Ensuring **new** buildings, vehicles, and facilities facilitate transportation, communications, and access
  - Eliminating, **to the extent possible**, architectural, transportation, and communication obstacles

**Also referenced in the BoK's resources:** the **ASEAN Human Rights Declaration** and the
**Arab Charter on Human Rights** (League of Arab States).

## 3C. National and Provincial Instruments

### The historical shift — the framing question

Civil rights protections for people with disabilities spread rapidly since the **1990s**. The US
adopted the **ADA in 1990**. In Europe, **thirty-three countries** adopted at least some form of
disability equality protections over a **25-year** period.

**Two drivers of the shift:**
1. **The move from the medical to the social model** of disability — driving the legal shift from a
   **welfare / social security law model** toward **anti-discrimination / equality law**
2. **The UN CRPD**

Most laws share an **equality focus** rather than a focus on fostering social welfare provisions.
Disability discrimination laws are considered a **truly new development** in disability policy. Laws
that treat disability as a **discrimination category** recognize people with disabilities as
**people with rights rather than problems**.

### The two legal approaches — reasonable accommodation vs. accessibility

| | **Reasonable accommodation** (individual accommodation) | **Accessibility** |
|---|---|---|
| Aim | Equal treatment on a **case-by-case** basis | Conditions met **regardless of anyone's disability status** |
| Trigger | A particular person's needs in a particular case | Universal — applies up front |
| Limit | Not required if it imposes a **disproportionate burden** | — |
| Example | **EU Employment Directive** | **Section 508 of the US Rehabilitation Act of 1973** |

**EU Employment Directive on reasonable accommodation:** employers shall take appropriate measures,
**where needed in a particular case**, to enable a person with a disability to have access to,
participate in, or advance in employment, or undergo training — **unless such measures would impose a
disproportionate burden**. The burden is **not disproportionate** when sufficiently remedied by
measures within the Member State's disability policy framework.

**Section 508 (accessibility approach):** when developing, procuring, maintaining, or using electronic
and information technology, agencies must ensure that **individuals with disabilities who are members
of the public** seeking information or services from a Federal department or agency have access to and
use of information and data **comparable to** that of members of the public without disabilities.

### The four national/provincial laws to know

**1. UK — Equality Act 2010**
- Brought together **multiple anti-discrimination laws** and strengthened them.
- Covers **England, Scotland, and Wales**.
- Requires "the exercise of certain functions to be with regard to the need to eliminate discrimination."
- Lists **disability as a protected characteristic**.
- Protects against **three forms of discrimination**:
  - **Direct discrimination**
  - **Discrimination arising from disability** — unfavourable treatment because of something arising
    as a **consequence** of a person's disability
  - **Indirect discrimination** — use of a criterion or practice that is discriminatory in relation to
    the protected characteristic
- Provisions on accessibility of transport, accessibility for disabled students, and **restricting when
  employers can ask job applicants about disability or health**.
- **Section 4 — the nine protected characteristics:** age · **disability** · gender reassignment ·
  marriage and civil partnership · pregnancy and maternity · race · religion or belief · sex ·
  sexual orientation

**2. USA — Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) of 1990**
- A **civil rights law**, putting disability protections on par with those for race, color, sex,
  national origin, age, and religion.
- **Guarantees equal opportunities in five areas:** public accommodations · employment · transportation ·
  state and local government services · telecommunications
- **Congressional findings worth knowing:** physical or mental disabilities **in no way diminish** a
  person's right to fully participate; society has historically **isolated and segregated** individuals
  with disabilities; discrimination persists in employment, housing, public accommodations, education,
  transportation, communication, recreation, institutionalization, health services, voting, and public
  services; **unlike** those facing race/color/sex/national origin/religion/age discrimination, people
  facing disability discrimination **often had no legal recourse**; people with disabilities occupy an
  **inferior status** and are severely disadvantaged socially, vocationally, economically, and educationally.
- **The Nation's four proper goals:** **equality of opportunity · full participation · independent
  living · economic self-sufficiency** ← memorize these four
- Continuing discrimination costs the United States **billions of dollars** in unnecessary expenses
  from dependency and non-productivity.

**3. Canada — Ontarians with Disabilities Act of 2001**
- Ensures the rights of people with disabilities to **equal opportunities and freedom from discrimination**.
- Preamble: the people of Ontario support the right of persons **of all ages** with disabilities to
  enjoy equal opportunity and participate fully in the life of the province.
- Notes that the **number of people with disabilities is expected to increase as the population ages**,
  since **incidence of disability increases with age**.
- Commits to a province where **no new barriers are created and existing ones are removed**, with
  responsibility resting on **every sector, region, government, organization, institution, association,
  and person** in Ontario.

**4. EU countries — Employment Equality Directive, 2000**
- Adopted **2000**; prohibits disability discrimination **in employment and occupation** (among other grounds).
- **All EU member states have implemented it** in national law.
- **Requires reasonable accommodation** to guarantee equal treatment.
- A general **Anti-discrimination Directive proposed in 2008** — to extend equal treatment **outside
  the labour market** — **has not been adopted.** As a result, general anti-discrimination and
  disability equality legislation **differs from country to country**.
- Example: in health care, member state legislation falls into **one of four groups** depending on
  whether it prohibits disability discrimination and/or requires reasonable accommodation:
  **both · prohibits discrimination only · requires reasonable accommodation only · neither**

## 3D. Domain-Specific and Procurement Laws

**Domain-specific laws** target specific technologies or economic sectors (e.g. transport) or
activities (e.g. procurement). They include laws developed specifically to ensure accessibility, **and**
general laws where accessibility forms a central part of the provisions.

### Domain-specific laws

| Law | Jurisdiction | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **Twenty-First Century Communications and Video Accessibility Act (CVAA), 2010** | US (FCC) | Updates federal communications law to increase access to **modern communications** — brings 1980s/1990s accessibility laws up to date with **digital, broadband, and mobile** innovations |
| **Air Carrier Access Act (ACAA)** | US (DOT) | Prohibits **discrimination on the basis of disability in air travel**. Applies to **all flights of U.S. airlines**, and to flights **to or from the US by foreign airlines** |
| **Air Carrier Access Amendments Act of 2017** | US Congress | Aircraft **must be designed** to accommodate individuals with disabilities and air carriers **must acquire aircraft** meeting broad accessibility standards |
| **EU Audiovisual Media Services Directive (AVMSD)** — Directive 2010/13/EU | EU | Coordinates national legislation on **all audiovisual media** — traditional TV broadcasts **and on-demand**. Requires services be made **continuously and progressively more accessible** through **proportionate measures**; requires **emergency information** via audiovisual media be accessible; encourages **accessibility action plans** |
| **European Electronic Communications Code** — Directive (EU) 2018/1972 | EU | Rules for high quality, secure, affordable telecoms. Requires **accessible format** service information and an **easily readable contract summary**; accessible **missing children and child helpline hotlines**; equal access to **emergency services**; affordable universal service with support and equipment for consumers with disabilities |
| **EU eIDAS Regulation** | EU | Requires that **trust services** — electronic signatures and end-user products providing trust services — be accessible to people with disabilities |

### Procurement laws

**Why they matter:** procurement laws are a tool for ensuring **user needs are accounted for when
procuring products and services**. They detail the accessibility standards products and services
should meet. Because large companies and public sector agencies handle **large budgets**,
well-developed accessibility requirements **may impact large parts of the market**.

**EU Public Procurement Directive (Directive 2014/24/EU) — three provisions:**
1. **Subject of the procurement:** for all procurement **intended for use by natural persons** (general
   public or the contracting authority's own staff), technical specifications **shall take into account
   accessibility criteria for people with disabilities or design for all users**. If another EU law
   already defines mandatory accessibility requirements, **those must be used**.
2. **Evaluation:** accessibility **may be considered in tender evaluation**. Economic operators proven
   unreliable through **violations of social obligations, including accessibility rules, may be excluded**.
3. **Electronic procurement documentation and communication** should take accessibility into due account.

> The specific procurement directive on **utilities and transport** and the directive on **concessions**
> have similar provisions. All have been implemented in **national legislation** of EU member states.

**US Federal Acquisition Regulation (FAR):** implements **Section 508** of the Rehabilitation Act for
electronic and information technology. When acquiring ICT, agencies must ensure that both **Federal
employees with disabilities** and **members of the public with disabilities** have access to and use
of information and data **comparable to** that of their non-disabled counterparts.

## 3E. Applying Accessibility Standards and Regulations to ICT

**The framing:** most countries have laws protecting civil rights of people with disabilities for
homes, parks, businesses, and educational facilities — **but this has not been the case with ICT
accessibility in all countries.**

### United States

- **Federal, state, and local government websites must meet Section 508 regulations**, following the
  **Section 508 Standards**, which are based on **WCAG 2.0**. ← note: **2.0**, not 2.1
- **The ADA does not include any legal standards for website accessibility** owned by private
  businesses or non-profits. **Nevertheless, they can be sued for lack of accessibility.**
- **Title III of the ADA** prohibits disability discrimination in the activities of **places of public
  accommodation** — businesses, schools, places of recreation, offices, medical buildings.
  **E-commerce, organizational websites, and public mobile applications fall under Title III.**
- **Enforcement:** the **US Department of Justice Civil Rights Division**; for **educational
  institutions**, the **US Department of Education Office for Civil Rights** — through **consent
  decrees and settlements**.

### European Union

**Web Accessibility Directive (Directive 2016/2102)** — applied from **September 2019**.
- Establishes accessibility requirements that **all member states' public sector bodies** must
  implement, maintain, and enforce for their **websites and mobile apps**.
- **Does not require procuring accessible solutions** (unlike the US) — but sites and apps **must be
  accessible when made available to users**.
- **Four requirements:**
  1. Ensuring websites and applications are accessible
  2. An **accessibility statement**
  3. A **method for users to report accessibility issues**
  4. A **link to enforcement procedures**

**EN 301 549** — the standard the Directive refers to.
- A **voluntary harmonized European standard**, originally created to provide accessibility
  requirements for **all ICT products and services**.
- **Annex A** lists requirements relevant to the Web Accessibility Directive. Being **harmonized**
  means following Annex A **presumes conformance** with the Directive.
- Because it's **voluntary**, public sector bodies may follow other standards — but then they may need
  to **prove** compliance with the law.
- **Annex A aligns with WCAG 2.1**, extending scope to **downloadable documents and mobile apps**, and
  adds requirements beyond WCAG on **biometrics, two-way voice communication, accessibility
  documentation, and support services**.

**European Accessibility Act (EAA)** — a directive **adopted 2019**, common rules **applied from 2025**.
- Covers products and services identified as **most important for people with disabilities**.
- Applies to **all private businesses** — manufacturers, representatives, importers, distributors —
  **except microenterprises**.
- **Products and services covered (memorize the list):** computers and operating systems · ATMs ·
  ticketing and check-in machines · smartphones · TV equipment related to digital television services ·
  telephony services and related equipment · access to audiovisual media services (television broadcast
  and related consumer equipment) · services related to **air, bus, rail, and waterborne** passenger
  transport · consumer banking services · **e-books** · **e-commerce**
- **Enforcement:** allows **NGOs, the responsible national authority, or other bodies to go to court on
  behalf of a person** under national law. Allows **penalties** for non-compliance. **Each member state
  is responsible for enforcement.**
- **Harmonized standards supporting the EAA** (standardization in progress): revised **EN 301 549**;
  revised **EN 17210** (accessibility and usability of the built environment); revised **EN 17161**
  (Design for All); a **new standard on non-ICT information related to products**; a **new standard for
  support services** (help desks, call centres, technical support, relay services, training); a **new
  standard for emergency communications** and answering them, including the single European emergency
  number **112**.

## 3F. Integrating ICT Accessibility Across the Organization

> **The single most quotable line in this section:** accessibility **"must be a program, not a
> project."** It must be approached **strategically and programmatically**, carried out as an
> **integral and ongoing activity**.

### W3C WAI: the four phases — Initiate, Plan, Implement, Sustain

**Initiate** — ground the effort in organizational culture, process, and practice. Align accessibility
with existing organizational approaches; develop and communicate clear, measurable objectives; engage
stakeholders for understanding and broad support.
- Learn the basics · Explore the current environment · Set objectives
- Develop the business case · Raise awareness · Gather support

**Plan** — ensures a clear assessment of required work, distribution of tasks, and continual follow-up.
- Create an **accessibility policy** · Assign responsibilities · Determine budget and resources
- Review environment · Review websites · Establish a **monitoring framework** · Engage with stakeholders

**Implement** — weave accessibility throughout the process to minimize overhead and improve quality.
**Prioritize quick wins** and communicate progress to increase commitment.
- Build skills and expertise · Integrate goals into policies · Assign tasks and support delivery
- **Evaluate early and regularly** · Prioritize issues · Track and communicate progress

**Sustain** — keep momentum; regular reviews of content, processes, and resources.
- Monitor websites · Engage with stakeholders · **Track standards and legislation**
- Adapt to new technologies · Incorporate user feedback

### European Agency for Special Needs & Inclusive Education Guidelines

**Seven recommendations:**
- Include an **accessibility statement** in the organization's long-term strategy
- Develop a strategy or plan for implementing accessible information
- **Make someone responsible** for implementing the plan — and provide them the required resources
- Plan an **incremental** implementation — "**be ambitious and modest at the same time**"
- **Embed** accessibility into information production and dissemination processes
- Provide information, education, and training on accessibility **for all staff**
- When **outsourcing**, ensure accessibility requirements are addressed and undergo a quality check

**Their three-part organizational implementation model — Policy → Plan → Practice:**

1. **Policy** — develop a long-term strategy recognizing all aspects of disability; publish an
   accessibility statement committing to accessible services and information; develop a **procurement
   policy** covering accessibility compliance.
2. **Plan** — develop an information accessibility plan that is **detailed and ambitious, but realistic
   and covers small steps**; ensure the responsible person or team has **authority and resources**.
3. **Practice** — conduct a **pilot** of the Guidelines; provide **awareness training for all staff**;
   provide training for content specialists on tools; produce **style guides and templates**; update
   work processes to embed accessibility; create information using the style guides; give **external
   providers** the Guidelines and compliance requirements; **conduct accessibility testing before
   releasing any service or publishing any information**.

### Maturity models

**Definition:** a tool for organisations to **measure progress and improvements** in a given area.
Several exist; **find one that best suits the culture and needs of your organization.**

**Business Disability Forum — Accessibility Maturity Model**
- Developed by the BDF's **Technology Taskforce** as a **self-assessment tool** for benchmarking progress.
- Based on the **Accessible Technology Charter**, which lists **ten commitments** to good ICT
  accessibility practices. Organizations make a **public commitment** by signing the Charter.
- Their definition of accessibility: ensuring people living with or acquiring a disability **can apply
  for jobs with us, be employed by us, and do business with us**.
- **Five levels — scale of 1 to 5:**
  1. **Informal** — no documentation or process in place
  2. **Defined** — documented but not actioned, or completed once
  3. **Repeatable** — process established and actioned consistently
  4. **Managed** — process monitored and improved; business as usual
  5. **Best practice** — innovate, improve, and share

**Capability Maturity Model (CMM), adapted** — developed by **Carnegie Mellon University**, an IT
process improvement model initially designed for **software development**, adaptable to measuring ICT
accessibility maturity.
1. **Initial** — capability is **ad hoc and unpredictable**; no stable environment for developing and
   maintaining accessible products, services, and information
2. **Repeatable** — policies in place for managing projects and procedures for ICT accessibility.
   Processes are: **practiced · documented · enforced · trained · measured · able to improve**
3. **Defined** — standard processes across the organization are **documented and integrated into a
   coherent whole**; used and changed as appropriate to help staff perform more effectively
4. **Managed** — the organization sets **quantitative quality goals**; processes include **well-defined
   and consistent measurements**
5. **Optimizing** — the entire organization focuses on **continuous process improvement**; weaknesses
   identified and processes strengthened **proactively to prevent defects**; innovations exploiting
   best practices identified and transferred organization-wide

> ⚠️ **The trap.** Both models have five levels and share three level *names* — but **"Defined" and
> "Repeatable" swap positions** between them. BDF: Informal → **Defined** → **Repeatable** → Managed →
> Best practice. CMM: Initial → **Repeatable** → **Defined** → Managed → Optimizing.
> (Note: BoK p.77 misprints the CMM list, nesting "Repeatable" under "Initial." The five levels above
> are the correct CMM sequence.)

### Management champions

**What they are:** people appointed from **key areas across the organization** as role models, with a
deep understanding of accessibility, who can assess accessibility within their area of responsibility
and lead improvement initiatives.

**Champions help to:**
- Build a vision and align implementation strategies
- Sustain ongoing commitment and collaboration
- Support the **programmatic** integration of accessibility throughout organizational process —
  **distinguishing it from discrete accessibility projects**
- Lead the adoption of an accessibility **maturity model**

### Evaluating for accessibility

**The core principle:** the W3C recommends **evaluating early and often** throughout design and
development. **It is easier and less costly to find and address accessibility issues early.**

- Ensure the product or service is **fit for purpose**; design for usability by people with
  disabilities; consider a "**born accessible**" approach
- Create **reusable design and code libraries**
- Use QA tools, methods, and protocols including accessibility evaluation tools — but **human
  evaluation is critical**
- Perform **formative, summative, and continuous** evaluations — test throughout the project lifecycle
  and any time new content is added or code updated
- **Include people with disabilities in evaluations**
- Use **outside expert analysis** where there are capability gaps, until in-house expertise is built

### Recruiting and hiring

**Recruiting and including people with disabilities:**
- Include the **disability inclusion statement** in job postings and recruitment pages
- Post openings on **disability-focused job boards**
- Ensure the **recruitment website and all postings are accessible**
- Promote at **disability-focused job fairs**
- Ensure **facilities are accessible** and provide reasonable accommodations including AT
- **Educate all employees, especially managers**, on working with employees with disabilities
- Ensure hiring and supervising managers understand accessibility obligations **and the organizational
  benefits** an employee with disabilities brings

**Recruiting people with digital accessibility skills** — define specific skills, which may include:
design with CSS/HTML/JavaScript · development with frameworks and libraries · accessibility testing
with evaluation tools and keyboard testing · using and testing with **screen reader software** ·
creating, testing, and remediating digital files · knowledge of standards such as **WCAG 2.1 and
PDF/UA**.

Post on industry job sites and professional networks: the **IAAP Career Center**, **a11yjobs**,
professional groups, and professional discussion groups and mailing lists.

### Communication management strategies

- Publish **accessibility standards for communications**
- Train communicators on using **people-first and plain language**
- Ensure accessibility of published **documents**, **websites**, and any media they contain or reference
- **Caption and describe videos** and other time-based communications
- Understand and design for **how people use assistive technologies**
- Capitalize on the increased understanding, influence, market, and **reputational potential**

### Legal and public relations implications

- Identify and become familiar with relevant **international, regional, national, and local** laws and standards
- **Assess your organization's legal liability**
- Ensure the organization **creates and follows an ICT accessibility plan**
- Ensure the plan **documents how products and services meet accessibility standards**
- Capitalize on the **positive public relations** accessibility brings
- **PR should consult legal, or risk and compliance, before publishing accessibility information** about
  the organization. In small organizations, people in roles of responsibility should seek guidance from
  laws and policies.

### Purchasing and public procurement — best practices

- **Verify product accessibility claims**
- **Verify a vendor's accessibility expertise and capacity**
- **Require product accessibility in contractual agreements**
- **Periodically review a vendor's product accessibility roadmap** for adherence
- **Leverage procurement policies and requirements to influence vendors**
