## 2A. Individualized Accommodations vs. Universal Design

### The core contrast

- **Universal design** — a product or service developed so that **as many people as possible can use
  it without needing adaptations**. Proactive, built in from the start, no specific target group.
- **Reasonable accommodation** (also called individual accommodation) — **specific modifications and
  adjustments** to an environment, product, or service to ensure equal access **in a specific case**.
  Reactive, individual, case-by-case.

**The relationship, stated precisely:** adopting universal design **does not prevent** the use of
adaptive devices or reasonable accommodation where needed. But universal design **reduces the extent
or need** for adaptations and personal assistance.

### Related concepts — same core idea, different names

Universal design's central idea has been developed into several related concepts:

- **Inclusive design**
- **Design for all**
- **Human-centered design**
- **Life-span design**

All share the idea of designing products, services, and environments so **as many people as possible
can use them without needing adaptations**.

**Regional note:** "**design for all**" is the term particularly used **in Europe** in universal
design and accessibility policy. European standard **EN 17161:2019 Design for All** supports
organisations in developing accessible products, goods, and services.

### Accessibility vs. usability vs. universal design — memorize the three-way distinction

All three aim to make products easier to use for everyone regardless of ability, but they **emphasize
different aspects**:

| Concept | Emphasis |
|---|---|
| **Accessibility** | Designing so **people with disabilities** have an **equivalent user experience** without barriers or discrimination |
| **Usability** | **Ease of use** and the user experience — but does **not always consider the needs of people with disabilities** |
| **Universal design** | Involving and including **everyone** to the greatest extent possible, **without specifying any particular target groups** |

> The tell: accessibility names disability explicitly. Usability doesn't necessarily consider it.
> Universal design deliberately names no group at all.

## 2B. Benefits of Accessibility

**For individuals:** accessibility enables participation in society — in major life activities like
education and employment, and in social activities necessary for health and happiness.

**For families and society:** benefit from the increased independence of more people, from the
contributions of people with a variety of abilities, from cost savings, and from improvements to the
built and digital world that improve access and usability **for everyone**.

**For organizations:** schools, employers, groups, and organizations of all types benefit from
**increased innovation and improved problem solving** brought by diversity through inclusion.

**Macro level:** there is evidence of a **global loss of gross domestic product (GDP)** from excluding
people with disabilities from the labor market.

## 2C. Web Accessibility and WCAG — the POUR principles

### What web accessibility means (W3C WAI)

Websites, tools, and technologies designed and developed so people with disabilities can use them —
specifically, so people can **perceive, understand, navigate, and interact with the Web**, and
**contribute to** it.

**Disabilities covered:** auditory · cognitive · neurological · physical · speech · visual

**Also benefits people without disabilities** — this list is exam-relevant:
- People using mobile phones, smart watches, smart TVs, and other small-screen or alternative-input devices
- **Older people** with changing abilities due to aging
- People with **temporary disabilities** — a broken arm, lost glasses
- People with **situational limitations** — bright sunlight, an environment where they can't listen to audio
- People on a **slow Internet connection**, or with limited or expensive bandwidth

> **Permanent / temporary / situational** is a framing worth holding onto — it shows up across the
> exam and is the strongest single argument in the business case for accessibility.

### The structure: 4 principles, 13 guidelines

**POUR** — Perceivable, Operable, Understandable, Robust.

#### Perceivable
- Provide **text alternatives** for non-text content
- Provide **captions and other alternatives** for multimedia
- Create content that can be **presented in different ways**, including by assistive technologies, **without losing meaning**
- Make it easier for users to **see and hear** content

#### Operable
- Make all functionality available **from a keyboard**
- Give users **enough time** to read and use content
- Do **not** use content that causes **seizures or physical reactions**
- Help users **navigate and find** content
- Make it easier to use **inputs other than a keyboard**

#### Understandable
- Make text **readable and understandable**
- Make content appear and operate in **predictable** ways
- Help users **avoid and correct mistakes**

#### Robust
- **Maximize compatibility** with current and future user tools

> **Count check:** 4 + 5 + 3 + 1 = **13 guidelines**. If you can reconstruct that arithmetic you can
> reconstruct the list.

> **Version gotcha:** the CPACC Content Outline says the exam covers **WCAG 2.1**. The BoK's Domain
> Two C body text refers to **WCAG 2.2**. The four principles and 13 guidelines are **identical in
> both** — that's what to know. Don't chase version-specific success criteria.

## 2D. Accessibility Principles for the Built Environment

**The goal:** plan, design, construct, and maintain buildings, public spaces, and transportation
systems accessible to all people. Need increases as disability rates grow and populations age.

**Two facts the BoK emphasizes:**
1. The **UD principles were originally developed for the built environment** and remain key guidelines there.
2. **The cost of not integrating UD into initial design and construction is often greater than the
   building's overall construction cost.** Consider needs from the start of the design phase.

### Minimum standards vs. best practice guidelines — a distinction that gets tested

- Rules and regulations **vary between countries**. Many countries have **minimum building standards**
  that must be met during design and construction.
- But **minimum standards often constitute accommodation rather than universal design**, which
  considers the needs of far more people.
- So countries often publish **best practice UD guidelines** to support their minimum standards.

> **Minimum standards are about compliance. Best practice guidelines aim for the best possible result
> to support inclusion and accessibility.**

**Areas of focus:**
- Access **in and out of** buildings — regulations often connect to **safety and evacuation**
- **Moving around** in buildings — furnishing, accessibility of facilities, perception of signs, accessible evacuation routes
- **Transport accessibility** — accessible routes, the outdoor environment, and access to means of transport
- Accessibility recommendations are often embedded in **broader policies**

## 2E. The Seven Principles of Universal Design

**Provenance — memorize this:** developed in **1997** by a working group of architects, product
designers, engineers, and environmental design researchers, led by the late **Ronald Mace** at
**North Carolina State University (NCSU)**.

**Definition (Ireland's National Disability Authority, Centre for Excellence in Universal Design):**
the design and composition of an environment so it can be **accessed, understood, and used to the
greatest extent possible by all people regardless of their age, size, ability or disability.** Not a
special requirement for a minority — **a fundamental condition of good design. Simply put, universal
design is good design.**

### The seven principles

**1. Equitable Use**
- Provide the **same means of use for all users**: identical whenever possible, equivalent when not
- **Avoid segregating or stigmatizing** any users
- Provisions for **privacy, security, and safety** equally available to all
- Make the design **appealing** to all users

**2. Flexibility in Use**
- Provide **choice in methods** of use
- Accommodate **right- or left-handed** access and use
- Facilitate the user's **accuracy and precision**
- Provide adaptability to the user's **pace**

**3. Simple and Intuitive Use**
- Eliminate **unnecessary complexity**
- Be consistent with **user expectations and intuition**
- Accommodate a wide range of **literacy and language skills**
- Arrange information **consistent with its importance**
- Provide effective **prompting and feedback** during and after task completion

**4. Perceptible Information**
- Use **different modes** (pictorial, verbal, tactile) for **redundant presentation** of essential information
- Provide adequate **contrast** between essential information and its surroundings
- **Maximize legibility** of essential information
- **Differentiate elements in ways that can be described** (make it easy to give instructions or directions)
- Provide **compatibility** with a variety of techniques or devices used by people with sensory limitations

**5. Tolerance for Error**
- Arrange elements to **minimize hazards and errors**: most-used elements most accessible; hazardous elements **eliminated, isolated, or shielded**
- Provide **warnings** of hazards and errors
- Provide **fail safe** features
- **Discourage unconscious action** in tasks that require vigilance

**6. Low Physical Effort**
- Allow user to maintain a **neutral body position**
- Use **reasonable operating forces**
- **Minimize repetitive** actions
- **Minimize sustained** physical effort

**7. Size and Space for Approach and Use**
- Provide a **clear line of sight** to important elements for any seated or standing user
- Make **reaching** to all components comfortable for any seated or standing user
- Accommodate variations in **hand and grip size**
- Provide **adequate space for the use of assistive devices**

> **Mnemonic:** **E**quitable, **F**lexible, **S**imple, **P**erceptible, **T**olerance, **L**ow
> effort, **S**ize and space → *"Every Fine Student Perceives That Learning Scales."*
> Or just remember the arc: **who can use it → how they use it → understanding it → perceiving it →
> surviving mistakes → physical cost → physical room.**

## 2F. Universal Design for Learning (UDL)

**What it is:** a set of guidelines accounting for various student needs and preferences when
designing instruction. Based on **principles of cognitive science** emphasizing **flexibility in the
presentation and demonstration of knowledge**. Published by **CAST**.

Emphasizes providing **multiple means** in instruction so students can **access and engage** with
material and **express the knowledge gained on their own terms**.

### The three principles — and the Why/What/How mapping

| Principle | Question | What it addresses |
|---|---|---|
| **Engagement** | the **WHY** of learning | Affect and motivation |
| **Representation** | the **WHAT** of learning | Perception and comprehension |
| **Action & Expression** | the **HOW** of learning | Navigation and demonstrating knowledge |

> The Why/What/How mapping is the single most likely UDL exam question. Lock it in.

Each guideline has **checkpoints** explaining how and why the instructor should provide options.

**Provide Multiple Means of Engagement** — learners differ markedly in how they can be engaged or
motivated. Variation comes from neurology, culture, personal relevance, subjectivity, and background
knowledge. Some are engaged by spontaneity and novelty; others are disengaged or frightened by it and
prefer strict routine. Some prefer working alone, others with peers.

Instructors should provide options for:
- **Recruiting learners' interest**
- **Learners to sustain their efforts**
- **Learners to self-regulate**

**Provide Multiple Means of Representation** — learners differ in how they perceive and comprehend
information. Sensory disabilities, learning disabilities, and language or cultural differences all
require different approaches. Learning and **transfer of learning** occur when multiple
representations are used, because they let students make connections **within** and **between** concepts.

Instructors should provide options for:
- **Perception** through different modalities
- **Language and symbols**
- **Comprehension** through design and presentation of information that **builds scaffolds for knowledge**

**Provide Multiple Means of Action and Expression** — learners differ in how they navigate a learning
environment and express what they know. Movement impairments (cerebral palsy), executive function
disorders, and language barriers all change how learners approach tasks. Some express themselves well
in written text but not speech, and vice versa. Action and expression require **strategy, practice,
and organization**.

Instructors should provide options for:
- **Interacting with tools and environments** that make learning physically accessible
- **Learners to express themselves and communicate**
- **Building learners' executive functions** through scaffolding

## 2F (cont.). Usability, User Experience, and User-Centered Design

### Usability vs. UX — the relationship

**UX is more comprehensive than usability.** Usability and accessibility are both necessary to
successful design, but *experience* includes **every interaction** a person has with a product:
becoming aware of it, acquiring or accessing it, using it for the first and subsequent times, and the
**level of trust and feeling of satisfaction** with it.

Per the Interaction Design Foundation: **usability is a sub-discipline of UX design.** They were once
used interchangeably; now understand that usability is an important **contribution** to UX, but is
**not the whole of the experience**.

### The three aspects of usability
1. Users should find it **easy and become proficient** when using a design interface
2. They should be able to **achieve their goal easily** through the design
3. They should be able to **learn the interface easily**, so return visits are just as easy or easier

### The five core areas of user experience — memorize the list
- **Usability** — a measure of a user's ability to arrive on a site, use it easily, and complete the desired task
- **Useful content** — enough information, in an easily digestible format, for informed decisions
- **Desirable / pleasurable content** — the best experiences come when the user forms an **emotional bond**
- **Accessibility** — a set of standards sites should conform to in order to be used by people with disabilities
- **Credibility** — the **trust** (levels of security and privacy) a website engenders

> UX comes as much from graphical design, interactive design, and content as it does from usability alone.

### User-centered design

**Definition:** an approach that puts the user at the **center of every stage** in the design and
development process, to ensure the target audience can use the product and to provide a better UX.

**Key elements:**
- **Involving users from the beginning and throughout** — via user research and user testing
- Taking an **iterative approach**, with testing after each stage to ensure designs and prototypes work in practice
- **User testing for accessibility can be included** in those iterative cycles
